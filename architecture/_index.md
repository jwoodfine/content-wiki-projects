## Styles and Movements Classical to Modern

Coverage opens with [[classical-architecture|Classical]] and [[gothic-architecture|Gothic]] tradition, through the highly decorative [[baroque-architecture|Baroque]] and the founding-era [[neoclassical-architecture|Neoclassical]] civic buildings of secondary-market downtowns, to the [[bauhaus|Bauhaus]] school and the [[modern-architecture|Modern]] style that established the suburban professional office building type. [[art-deco|Art Deco]] and [[vernacular-architecture|Vernacular]] round out the period: decorative 1920s–30s commercial streets, and the local-material, local-convention forms that define most small-city commercial stock.

<!-- AUTO-GENERATED MEMBERSHIP: DO NOT EDIT BELOW — regenerate from index_group: styles-and-movements-classical-to-modern -->
- [[classical-architecture]] — Architectural tradition derived from ancient Greek and Roman principles — symmetry, column orders, proportion, and formal composition — that dominated Western institutional and commercial construction from the Renaissance through the early 20th century.
- [[baroque-architecture]] — Highly decorative and theatrically dramatic architectural style originating in early 17th-century Italy that spread across Europe and its colonies — defining the civic and ecclesiastical building fabric of most major European city centres between approximately 1600 and 1750.
- [[gothic-architecture]] — Medieval style of pointed arch, rib vault, and flying buttress, revived in the 19th century as Gothic Revival in the churches and civic buildings of secondary markets.
- [[neoclassical-architecture]] — Architectural movement of the 18th and early 19th centuries reviving Greek and Roman forms, defining the founding-era banks and civic buildings of secondary market downtowns.
- [[bauhaus]] — A German design school (1919–1933) whose aesthetic of functional abstraction, industrial materials, and geometric rigour became the dominant formal vocabulary of modern commercial and institutional architecture worldwide.
- [[modern-architecture]] — Dominant mid-twentieth-century commercial style — structural frame, curtain wall, flat roof, open plan — that established the suburban professional office building type.
- [[art-deco]] — Decorative style of the 1920s and 1930s — geometric ornament, stepped massing, rich surface materials — defining downtown commercial streets in North American secondary cities.
- [[vernacular-architecture]] — Building forms shaped by local materials, climate, and community convention rather than formal design training — a category that defines the character of most small-city commercial and mixed-use stock in secondary markets.

## Styles and Movements Postmodern to Contemporary

[[postmodern-architecture|Postmodernism]] revived historical reference and ornament in the brick-veneer commercial inventory of secondary-market corridors; [[deconstructivism|Deconstructivism]] and [[high-tech-architecture|High-Tech]] pushed fragmentation and exposed structural systems into cultural institutions and adaptable corporate floor plates. [[neomodern|Neomodern]] returned to rationalism as the dominant Class A office-tower style since the 1990s, while [[neo-futurism|Neo-Futurism]] and [[new-classical-architecture|New Classical]] pull in opposite directions — expressive, computer-aided form in transit infrastructure, and a continuation of the classical orders in traditional town design. [[contemporary-architecture|Contemporary practice]] closes the sequence: no single dominant style, spanning all of these approaches at once.

<!-- AUTO-GENERATED MEMBERSHIP: DO NOT EDIT BELOW — regenerate from index_group: styles-and-movements-postmodern-to-contemporary -->
- [[postmodern-architecture]] — Architectural movement of the 1980s and 1990s reviving historical reference and ornament, producing the brick-veneer suburban commercial inventory of secondary market corridors.
- [[deconstructivism]] — Postmodern architectural movement of the 1980s characterised by fragmentation, non-rectilinear geometry, and the deliberate disruption of conventional structural logic — appearing primarily in cultural institutions, museums, and memorial architecture.
- [[high-tech-architecture]] — Late modernist style expressing structural and mechanical systems on the building exterior, yielding adaptable open floor plates for corporate, medical, and technology tenants.
- [[neomodern]] — Architectural movement returning to rationalism and simplified form after postmodernism — dominant style of Class A office towers in North American and European CBDs since the 1990s.
- [[neo-futurism]] — Architectural movement applying advanced technology and computer-aided design to expressive, non-rectilinear forms in transit infrastructure and cultural anchors.
- [[new-classical-architecture]] — Contemporary movement continuing classical and traditional architecture through the classical orders, traditional town design, and context-sensitive construction.
- [[contemporary-architecture]] — The architecture of the 21st century — no single dominant style, characterised by global production, computer-aided design, advanced materials, and a proliferation of competing design philosophies spanning high-tech, sculptural, neo-classical, and ecological approaches.

## Style and Place

Two articles connect style to place directly. [[new-urbanism|New Urbanism]] describes the planning framework behind mixed-use, transit-connected neighbourhoods — the framework that formalizes many of the land-use conditions that support co-location. [[contextual-architecture|Contextual Architecture]] covers designing a new building to its existing surroundings rather than to its own internal logic.

<!-- AUTO-GENERATED MEMBERSHIP: DO NOT EDIT BELOW — regenerate from index_group: style-and-place -->
- [[new-urbanism]] — A design movement that reintroduces mixed-use, transit-connected, pedestrian-scaled neighbourhoods as an alternative to automobile-dependent suburban development — the planning framework that formalizes many of the land-use conditions that support co-location.
- [[contextual-architecture]] — A design approach that gives priority to the relationship between a new building and its existing surroundings — scale, material, massing, and use — rather than to the building's own internal formal logic.
